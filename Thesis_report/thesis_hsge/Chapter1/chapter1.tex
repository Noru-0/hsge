\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

This chapter (1) motivates the use of small language models as web agents,
(2) states the problem addressed by this thesis, (3) analyzes its challenges,
(4) lists our objectives and contributions, (5) delimits the scope, and
(6) outlines the structure of the thesis.

\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-context}

Consider a developer who starts every morning with the same ritual on a
project-hosting site: open the issue tracker, find the newly reported bugs,
label them, and assign the urgent ones. Nothing in this workflow requires
human judgment beyond what is written on the screen --- yet it consumes
attention daily, and the same is true of countless workflows in online
shops, forums, and administration dashboards. The browser is the universal
interface to these services, which makes a software agent that operates
websites \emph{through their user interface} one of the most broadly
applicable forms of automation.

Large language models (LLMs) have recently made such web agents plausible.
Given a textual rendering of a page and a goal, an LLM can decide what to
click and type next, and agents built this way can complete realistic tasks
on real websites. The capability, however, currently lives almost entirely
in very large proprietary models accessed over an API. This dependence is
costly in several compounding ways. Every step of every task is a paid API
call, and agent workloads take many steps; latency is bounded by a remote
service; the full content of every visited page --- possibly including
private or internal data --- is sent to a third party; and the model itself
is a fixed commodity that the operator cannot inspect or improve. For
organizations that would benefit most from web automation, these constraints
often rule out the strongest models entirely.

Small language models (SLMs) --- models of roughly ten billion parameters or
fewer that can be self-hosted on a single GPU --- invert every one of these
constraints: inference is cheap, local, and private, and the model's weights
are the operator's to fine-tune. What they lack is competence. Even
\emph{large} models find realistic web tasks hard: on the WebArena benchmark
of self-hosted, fully functional websites, a GPT-4-based agent completed
about 14\% of tasks, against roughly 78\% for humans~\cite{zhou2024webarena}.
% TODO(verify): exact figures 14.41% and 78.24% from the WebArena paper.
Out of the box, small models fare far worse. The standard remedy ---
supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations of the target behavior --- runs
into a data problem: goal-annotated interaction trajectories on the target
website rarely exist, and producing them with human annotators is expensive,
slow, and site-specific.

This thesis attacks that data problem at its root. If an automated pipeline
can \emph{explore} a website on its own, \emph{invent} meaningful tasks
grounded in what it finds, \emph{verify} which of its attempts actually
succeeded, and compile the results into training data, then the cost of
specializing an SLM to a website drops from a human annotation campaign to a
matter of compute. The central idea of our approach is that the pipeline
should not merely wander: it should build a \emph{graph} of what it has
learned the site can do --- verified skills and their preconditions and
effects --- and then \emph{compose} that graph into long-horizon, semantic
training tasks that no single page would ever suggest.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

The problem addressed by this thesis is the following.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Given} (i) a live website with no task annotations, exposed through a
standard browser, and (ii) a pretrained small language model,
\textbf{produce} a fine-tuned version of that model that completes
natural-language tasks on the website, \textbf{without} any human-authored
demonstrations or task labels.
\end{quote}

The agent interface follows the standard web-agent formulation: at each step
the model observes the current URL and the page's accessibility tree (a
textual rendering of the visible interface), and it emits one action from a
fixed action space --- clicking, typing, selecting, navigating, or declaring
the task finished or infeasible. A \emph{trajectory} is the sequence of
observation--action pairs produced while pursuing one goal.

The crux of the problem is the intermediate artifact. Fine-tuning needs a
dataset $D = \{(g_i, \tau_i)\}$ of goals paired with successful trajectories,
so the real question is a \emph{data-generation} one: how can goals $g_i$
that are meaningful on this website, and trajectories $\tau_i$ that verifiably
achieve them, be manufactured automatically? Quality is judged by the end
use: the fine-tuned model is evaluated by its \emph{task success rate} on the
official, programmatically checked task suites of the WebArena
benchmark~\cite{zhou2024webarena}, measured against the same model before
fine-tuning.

\section{Challenges}
\label{sec:intro-challenges}

Five properties of the setting make the problem hard.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Exploration on a live website.} Real sites expose thousands
  of near-duplicate pages (every product, post, and profile shares a
  template), pages fail and time out, and actions have side effects on
  shared, persistent state. Naive crawling drowns in duplicates or dies on
  the first flaky page.
  \item \textbf{Meaningful tasks, not random walks.} Random interaction
  sequences are easy to generate and nearly worthless as demonstrations.
  Useful training tasks must correspond to goals a person might actually
  pursue --- and the most valuable ones span multiple pages and capabilities.
  \item \textbf{Verification.} An agent that generates its own tasks also
  grades its own attempts. Unverified ``successes'' are not merely wasted
  data: fine-tuning on them actively teaches the model to declare victory
  prematurely.
  \item \textbf{Long-horizon composition.} A task proposal grounded in a
  single page is inherently short-horizon. Producing legitimate multi-step
  goals requires knowing how capabilities discovered on \emph{different}
  pages connect --- knowledge no single observation contains.
  \item \textbf{Faithful transfer into a small model.} Whatever the pipeline
  collects must reach the model without distribution shift: the training
  prompts, the serving configuration, and the evaluation harness must match
  the live agent exactly, or the fine-tune optimizes for the wrong
  distribution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives and Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contrib}

The thesis pursues five objectives, matched one-to-one to the challenges
above.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{O1.}] Build an exploration pipeline that autonomously
  collects interaction trajectories from an unannotated website, robust to
  duplicate structure and page failures, and safe on shared state.
  \item[\textbf{O2.}] Generate \emph{verified, long-horizon semantic tasks}
  by discovering reusable skills, lifting them into planning operators, and
  chaining those operators over a graph.
  \item[\textbf{O3.}] Curate the collected trajectories into a fine-tuning
  dataset through automated quality gates, under strict train--serve prompt
  parity.
  \item[\textbf{O4.}] Fine-tune a small language model on the resulting
  dataset and evaluate it on the official WebArena task suites of four sites.
  \item[\textbf{O5.}] Distill the engineering experience of operating LLM
  agents on live websites into reusable lessons and best practices.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:contrib} separates what this thesis builds on from what it
contributes. In brief, the contributions are: HSGE, a graph-based
exploration-and-composition pipeline with a two-tier task hierarchy
(LLM-proposed low-level tasks for exploration; operator-graph-generated
semantic tasks for long-horizon data); an automated curation stack (episode
judging and thought re-annotation) feeding a prompt-parity dataset builder; a
fine-tuned 9B web-agent model with its collected dataset; an evaluation of
that model on official WebArena suites; and an experience report on running
such pipelines reliably.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

The thesis deliberately bounds its claims as follows. The environment is the
self-hosted WebArena site family; evaluation targets the official task
suites of four sites --- Shopping, Shopping Admin, GitLab, and Reddit ---
using their programmatic evaluators. The training method is supervised
fine-tuning with LoRA only; reinforcement learning and preference
optimization are out of scope. The target model is a single
$\sim$9B-parameter SLM, and all tasks are in English. Authentication uses
the benchmark's standard test accounts with cached sessions; CAPTCHA solving
and other anti-bot circumvention are out of scope, as is any interaction
with websites other than the self-hosted benchmark instances.
